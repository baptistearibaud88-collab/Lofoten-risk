03

Independent summits, shared weather

Each massif starts at sea level and comes back to it: between two summits, you come down to the edge of a fjord, a road or a village. The traverse is a sequence of 15 short, independent climbs, each with its own way out.

Specific risk

Specific to one massif: scree, an invisible trail, a rock band. It says nothing about the next massif.

Systematic risk

Shared by all the massifs of the moment: the weather. No return to sea level wipes it out.

04

Sensitivities: what slows me down, and what does not

A regression on the 604 segments isolates the effect of each factor, all else being equal. Each bar gives the change in time taken, with its 95% confidence interval.

Marginal effect on the time of a segment
Correlation matrix (Spearman, by outing)

How to read

A bar to the right of zero slows down, a bar to the left speeds up. If the whisker crosses zero, the effect cannot be told apart from chance (n.s.). In the matrix, red = both factors rise together, blue = one rises when the other falls.

Method

Ordinary least squares on ln(actual time / Tobler), with slope (absolute value: up or down), pack, terrain grade, gusts, HRV of the previous night and steep descent (> 15%). Effects converted into %: eβ − 1.

Key takeaway

What the heart says

The regression finds no effect of fatigue on pace. Yet fatigue exists. Heart rate variability (HRV), measured every night, reflects recovery: 0% is an ordinary night (45 ms, my usual value outside the traverse). Below, the length of each night.

How to read

The dotted line (0%) is the usual HRV; the grey band, the normal zone at ±10%. In green, above usual; in red, below. A curve that stays under the band signals fatigue building up.

Key takeaway

Rain comes in streaks: 2026 against 30 summers

A total does not tell everything: fine days come together, and so do rainy ones. The real question is whether I hit a rare week, an exceptional rain episode, or simply a favourable calendar.

Rain the next day
ERA5 · central point · June–July 1996–2025
2026 among 1,200 nine-day windows

How to read

On the left, the chance of rain on a given day, depending on whether it rained the day before. If days were independent, the two bars would be equal. On the right, each histogram sorts 1,200 nine-day windows drawn from the summers of 1996–2025; the red bar is the 2026 bin, and the percentile says what share of windows does less.

Method

ERA5 (Open-Meteo), central point, daily rain from 1996 to 2025. A rainy day gets at least 1 mm. Persistence measured over the 1,800 day-to-day transitions in June and July. Nine-day windows, like the traverse, with a start every day from 1 June to 10 July: 30 summers × 40 dates = 1,200 windows, the same as in parts 07 and 09.
